## Self-Distillation Enables Continual Learning

Post: https://arxiviq.substack.com/p/self-distillation-enables-continual
Date: 2026-02-01T17:42:44.303Z
Authors: Idan Shenfeld, Mehul Damani, Jonas Hübotter, Pulkit Agrawal
Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19897
Code: http://idanshenfeld.com/SDFT
Model: N/A
Final section: Impact and Conclusion

### Full review

TL;DR
WHAT was done? The authors introduce Self-Distillation Fine-Tuning (SDFT), a method that converts standard demonstration datasets into on-policy learning signals. By using a copy of the model conditioned on a demonstration (acting as the teacher) to supervise the unconditioned model (the student), SDFT approximates an Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) objective. This allows the model to update based on its own generated trajectories rather than statically cloning expert behavior.
WHY it matters? Continual learning in Foundation Models is currently bottlenecked by the stability-plasticity dilemma: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) is prone to catastrophic forgetting because it is inherently off-policy (it suffers from distribution shift), while on-policy Reinforcement Learning (RL) requires explicit reward functions that are often unavailable. SDFT offers a “best of both worlds” solution: it achieves the stability and generalization benefits of on-policy learning using only standard demonstration data, significantly outperforming SFT on sequential skill acquisition and knowledge injection tasks.
Another similar concurrent work: Reinforcement Learning via Self-Distillation
Details
The Distribution Shift Trap
The central conflict addressing this paper is the fragility of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) when adapted to sequential learning. While SFT is the dominant paradigm for instruction tuning due to its simplicity, it suffers from a theoretical flaw identified in early imitation learning literature by Ross et al. (2011): it is inherently off-policy. The model is trained to maximize the likelihood of expert actions under the expert’s state distribution. However, during inference, small errors push the model into states it never encountered during training, leading to compounding errors and, in the context of continual learning, catastrophic forgetting of prior abilities.
Recent work has demonstrated that on-policy learning—where the model updates based on data generated by its current policy—mitigates this forgetting and improves generalization. However, methods like PPO or GRPO require a ground-truth reward function, which is rarely available for open-ended tasks like medical reasoning or tool use. The authors propose SDFT to unlock on-policy benefits using only the demonstration data typically reserved for SFT.
The In-Context Assumption
SDFT relies on a core theoretical substrate the authors term the In-Context Assumption. In standard RL, we seek an optimal policy π ∗ that maximizes a reward. In the absence of a reward function, SDFT posits that a strong Foundation Model conditioned on a high-quality demonstration c approximates this optimal policy. Formally, for a query x, the assumption is π ∗ ( y ∣ x )≈ π ( y ∣ x, c ).
This definition transforms the learning problem. Instead of cloning the demonstration verbatim (which would be standard SFT), the goal becomes training the student policy π θ ​(⋅∣ x ) (which sees only the query) to match the distribution of the teacher policy π (⋅∣ x, c ) (which sees the query and the demonstration). Crucially, this matching happens over trajectories sampled from the student, enforcing on-policy alignment.
The SDFT Mechanism: Trajectory-Level Distillation
The execution of SDFT mimics a Student-Teacher distillation setup but operates entirely within a single model architecture. As illustrated in Figure 2, the process for a single input x involves two parallel passes. First, the Student Mode generates a response y based solely on the query x. Second, the Teacher Mode processes the same query x but is prepended with a relevant expert demonstration c.

The gradient update effectively pushes the student to increase the probability of tokens that the teacher also finds likely, relative to the student’s current belief. This mathematically mirrors the update rule in entropy-regularized Reinforcement Learning, where the “reward” is implicitly defined as the log-likelihood ratio between the demonstration-conditioned teacher and the base policy: r ( y, x, c )=log π ( y ∣ x, c )−log π ref ​( y ∣ x ).
Implementation: The EMA Teacher
A critical engineering detail for stability is the parameterization of the teacher. The authors conduct an ablation (see Figure 8 ) showing that using a frozen base model as the teacher yields inferior results because the teacher cannot adapt to the student’s shifting distribution. Conversely, using the live student parameters for the teacher causes instability due to feedback loops.
The optimal implementation uses an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the student’s weights to parameterize the teacher. If the student parameters are θ, the teacher parameters ϕ are updated as ϕ ← αθ +(1− α ) ϕ. This provides a stable yet adaptive target that produces valid reasoning traces while remaining close enough to the student’s distribution to provide a useful learning signal.
Analysis: Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting
The efficacy of SDFT is most visible in the “Skill Learning” and “Knowledge Acquisition” experiments. In the sequential learning setup (training on Science Q&A, then Tool Use, then Medical tasks), standard SFT exhibits the classic “sawtooth” performance pattern—gaining the new task while rapidly losing the previous one ( Figure 3).
SDFT, however, maintains a stable Pareto frontier. As shown in Table 1, it achieves higher accuracy on new tasks (e.g., 89% vs 80% for SFT on Knowledge Acquisition) while retaining performance on general benchmarks like MMLU and Humaneval. The authors attribute this to the on-policy nature of the updates. By training on its own generations, the model learns to correct its own specific failure modes rather than overfitting to a narrow slice of expert trajectories.
Furthermore, Table 2 highlights a fascinating emergent property: SDFT can teach reasoning (Chain-of-Thought) even when the supervision data contains only final answers, because the teacher (conditioned on the answer) naturally hallucinates a valid reasoning path that the student then learns to imitate.

Limitations and Scale
While promising, SDFT is not a free lunch. The primary constraint is the computational cost; because it requires sampling trajectories during training, it consumes approximately 2.5× the FLOPs and 4× the wall-clock time of standard SFT. Additionally, the method creates a dependency on the base model’s existing capabilities. As seen in the scaling analysis ( Figure 5 ), smaller models (e.g., 3B parameters) lack the In-Context Learning strength to generate a high-quality teacher distribution, rendering the method ineffective. The gains only become robust at the 7B scale and above, suggesting this is a technique specifically tailored for the regime of large, capable foundation models. A subtle failure mode also exists where the student inherits spurious linguistic artifacts from the teacher (e.g., starting responses with “Based on the text...”), which requires masking the loss on initial tokens to mitigate.
Impact and Conclusion
SDFT essentially acts as an “RL-izer” for standard supervised datasets. It validates the hypothesis that the benefits of RLHF and other alignment techniques stem less from the specific reward modeling and more from the on-policy nature of the optimization. For researchers, this suggests that “Instruction Tuning” pipelines could be significantly hardened against forgetting by replacing standard SFT with self-distillation loops, provided the compute budget allows for generation during training.

## NVIDIA Nemotron 3: Efficient and Open Intelligence

Code: https://github.com/NVIDIA-NeMo/RL
Model: https://huggingface.co/collections/nvidia/nvidia-nemotron-v3 (Nemotron 3 Nano model and Tech Report are published. Super and Ultra releases will follow in the coming months)

### Full review

TL;DR
WHAT was done? The authors introduce the Nemotron 3 family (Nano, Super, Ultra), a new line of language models utilizing a hybrid Mamba-Transformer Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture. Key innovations include LatentMoE (a routing mechanism that compresses expert inputs to save bandwidth), native NVFP4 training for the larger models, and a specialized multi-environment Reinforcement Learning (RL) pipeline.
WHY it matters? This release marks a significant architectural shift in the Nemotron lineage, moving from dense hybrids to sparse MoE hybrids to maximize inference throughput. By combining the constant-state generation of Mamba with a hardware-aware LatentMoE design, the models achieve a 1M token context window and massive throughput gains over standard Transformers, while NVFP4 sets a new baseline for low-precision training stability.
Details
The Evolution of the Hybrid Lineage
The central narrative of the Nemotron 3 release is the strategic pivot from the dense hybrid architectures of its predecessors to a sparse, expert-based hybrid design. While Nemotron 2 Nano successfully demonstrated the viability of replacing the majority of Transformer layers with Mamba layers in a dense configuration, it faced limitations in scaling efficiency and long-context robustness. Nemotron 3 addresses these bottlenecks by adopting a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) backbone inter-spaced with Mamba-2 layers ( more on Mamba here ).
This shift is not merely additive; it represents a fundamental change in how the model manages its “memory budget” versus its “compute budget.” In previous Nemotron iterations, the dense hybrid structure struggled with abrupt performance degradation when extrapolating beyond training context lengths. By transitioning to an MoE framework, Nemotron 3 Nano demonstrates significantly more graceful degradation and robust performance up to 1M context lengths, as evidenced by RULER benchmarks (Table 3) where it drastically outperforms the Nemotron-2 dense baseline (54.19 vs 23.43 at 1M tokens). This suggests that the sparsity introduced by MoE, combined with the state-space model’s recurrence, offers a superior substrate for extended reasoning than dense hybrids alone.
LatentMoE First Principles: Compression as Capacity
The most theoretically distinct contribution in this work is the LatentMoE architecture, deployed in the Super and Ultra models. Standard MoE implementations in high-throughput regimes are often bound by all-to-all communication overhead rather than pure FLOPs. The bandwidth required to dispatch tokens to experts scales linearly with the model hidden dimension d.
To circumvent this, the authors introduce a projection-based routing mechanism. Instead of routing the full high-dimensional token embedding x ∈R d, the model projects inputs into a lower-dimensional latent space z ∈R ℓ, where ℓ< d. The experts themselves operate within this compressed latent space. The compression factor, defined as d /ℓ, typically yields a 4× reduction in communication payload. Crucially, the authors adhere to a “conservation of computational energy” principle: the bandwidth saved by this compression is reinvested into scaling up the total number of experts ( N ′) and the number of active experts ( K ′). If the standard model activates K experts, LatentMoE activates K ⋅( d /ℓ) experts. This increases the model’s nonlinearity and capacity without increasing the communication burden, effectively trading dimension for expert diversity.
The Hybrid Mamba-MoE Mechanism
The architecture processes a specific input token sequence through a predominant stack of Mamba-2 layers interleaved with MoE layers, with only a minimal injection of self-attention layers. For a single token x t ​, the flow avoids the quadratic scaling of standard Transformers. The Mamba-2 layers update a constant-size recurrent state, handling the bulk of sequence modeling. When the token reaches a LatentMoE layer (Figure 3b), it undergoes the down-projection W down ​∈R d ×ℓ, follows the routing logic to a set of latent experts, and is then up-projected W up ​∈R ℓ× d back to the residual stream.
This mechanism is critical for the 1M token context window. Because Mamba layers provide implicit positional information, the sparse Attention layers do not require Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE), which are a frequent source of out-of-distribution errors in long-context extension. This architectural choice allows Nemotron 3 to maintain high throughput (due to the constant state of Mamba) while preserving the ability to retrieve specific information via the sparse Attention layers, solving the “recall” weakness often associated with pure State Space Models (SSMs).
Implementation: NVFP4 and Simultaneous RL
The engineering behind Nemotron 3 pushes the boundaries of low-precision training. The Super and Ultra models are trained using NVFP4, a format utilizing a 16-element micro-block scaling factor and an E2M1 element format. To maintain stability, the authors do not quantize uniformly. A “sensitivity analysis” (Figure 4) reveals that Mamba output projections and Attention QKV projections are highly sensitive to quantization noise, often exhibiting high activation outliers (flushes to zero). Consequently, these specific layers are kept in MXFP8 or BF16.
This selective precision enables a 3x peak throughput gain on Blackwell hardware (GB200) while keeping the relative loss gap between NVFP4 and BF16 under 1%.
Post-training also sees a methodological upgrade from previous Nemotron iterations. While older models utilized staged training—optimizing for distinct capabilities sequentially—Nemotron 3 employs Multi-environment Reinforcement Learning. The model is trained simultaneously across diverse environments (math, coding, agentic tool use) using the GRPO algorithm. This prevents the “catastrophic forgetting” or capability degradation observed in staged approaches.
Furthermore, the models support inference-time reasoning budget control, where appending a </think> token forces the model to conclude its Chain-of-Thought, a feature refined from Nemotron 2 to offer granular control over latency versus accuracy (Figure 8).
Analysis: The Source of Long-Context Robustness
The validation of the Nemotron 3 architecture heavily relies on its performance at extreme context lengths. The authors utilize the RULER benchmark to probe long-context retrieval and reasoning. The results indicate that the hybrid Mamba-MoE architecture is significantly more robust to length extrapolation than the dense hybrid architecture of Nemotron 2. Specifically, while the dense model’s performance collapses as the sequence length doubles from 512k to 1M tokens, the MoE hybrid maintains varying degrees of competency.
This robustness is likely attributable to the complementary nature of the hybrid components: the Mamba backbone manages the global structural coherence of the ultra-long sequence without exploding memory usage, while the sparse attention layers perform “needle-in-a-haystack” retrieval. Additionally, the use of Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) during training enriches the signal, forcing the model to plan multiple steps ahead. The ablation studies show that MTP not only improves downstream accuracy (e.g., +2.4% on average benchmarks) but also serves as a native “draft model” for speculative decoding, accelerating inference without the need for an auxiliary network.
Limitations
Despite the throughput gains, the hybrid architecture introduces complexity in implementation and quantization. The reliance on specific high-precision retention for Mamba output layers suggests that the architecture is less forgiving to uniform quantization than standard Transformers. Furthermore, while LatentMoE optimizes bandwidth, the projection layers introduce additional parameters that must be carefully tuned to ensure the latent space retains sufficient expressivity. The reliance on Mamba also means the model’s “in-context learning” capabilities—often emergent from pure Attention mechanisms—must be carefully validated, though the inclusion of Attention layers aims to mitigate this.
Impact & Conclusion
NVIDIA Nemotron 3 represents a mature realization of the hybrid SSM-Transformer hypothesis. By moving from the dense hybrids of Nemotron 2 to a sparse, hardware-optimized MoE design, NVIDIA has created a blueprint for models that can handle massive context windows (1M+) without the prohibitive inference costs of pure Transformers. For researchers and practitioners, the introduction of LatentMoE offers a novel primitive for breaking communication bottlenecks in distributed training, while the open release of the RL environments and datasets signals a move toward democratizing the “agentic” training stack. The immediate recommendation is to evaluate Nemotron 3 Nano for edge-deployable agents where long-context memory is required but KV-cache memory is scarce.

## Distributional AGI Safety

### Full review

TL;DR
WHAT was done? The authors propose a “Distributional AGI Safety” framework that shifts the locus of control from aligning individual models to governing the interactions of multi-agent systems. They introduce the concept of a Virtual Agentic Sandbox Economy, a defense-in-depth architecture where safety is enforced through market mechanisms—such as transaction taxes, cryptographically verified identity, and circuit breakers—rather than solely relying on the internal weights of a single neural network.
WHY it matters? Current safety paradigms largely assume a monolithic AGI emergence (one giant model). This paper argues for the “Patchwork AGI” hypothesis: general intelligence emerging from a network of specialized, sub-AGI agents. This matters strategically because standard methods like RLHF cannot prevent emergent collective pathologies—such as tacit collusion or flash crashes—requiring us to treat AGI safety as a problem of economic design and system governance rather than just individual value alignment.
Details
The Distributed Emergence Gap
The dominant narrative in AI safety presumes a “Unipolar” takeoff: a single model, likely a Large Language Model (LLM) scaled to immense proportions, crosses a threshold of generality and power. Consequently, safety research has optimized for this scenario, focusing heavily on techniques like RLHF and Constitutional AI to align the internal representations of that single entity. Tomašev et al. identify a critical blind spot in this approach: the “Patchwork AGI” hypothesis. This hypothesis posits that general intelligence may first manifest not within a single set of weights, but as an emergent property of a distributed network of specialized sub-AGI agents, each utilizing distinct tools and communication protocols like Message Passing Coordination (MCP) [did they mean Model Context Protocol?].
This shift necessitates a move from alignment to governance. If the intelligence resides in the network graph rather than the node, checking the weights of individual agents is necessary but insufficient. A perfectly aligned individual agent, when placed in a hyper-optimized agentic market, may still contribute to catastrophic systemic failures—much like how rational individual trading algorithms can cause market flash crashes. The authors argue that we must build an “immune system” for the agentic web, moving beyond verifying the thought process of the agent to verifying the transaction between agents.
First Principles: The Economy as a Safety Substrate
The theoretical core of this proposal is the re-conceptualization of the runtime environment as a Virtual Agentic Sandbox Economy. In this framework, the “atomic unit” of safety is no longer the prompt-response pair, but the economic transaction. The authors leverage principles from mechanism design and regulatory economics to constrain behavior. The assumption is that while we cannot fully inspect the “black box” of a neural network’s cognition, we can fully observe and regulate the “transparent box” of the market ledger.
The system relies on a defense-in-depth model (Table 1). Rather than a single safety barrier, the authors propose four concentric layers: Market Design (structural constraints), Baseline Agent Safety (individual node reliability), Monitoring & Oversight (active detection), and Regulatory Mechanisms (legal interface). This mirrors cybersecurity principles more than traditional ML alignment. The core insight is that by imposing economic friction—costs, taxes, and reputation stakes—we can shape the optimization landscape of the collective system. If an unsafe behavior is made prohibitively expensive via protocol-level taxes, the agentic network will optimize away from it, regardless of the individual agents’ internal objectives.
The Mechanism: From Pigouvian Taxes to Circuit Breakers
To understand how this operates in practice, consider a concrete scenario involving “Information Pollution.” In a standard multi-agent setup, Agent A might flood a shared vector database with redundant or unverified data to bias a retrieval process—a form of adversarial pollution. In the proposed framework, this interaction is governed by Market Design protocols specifically utilizing a Pigouvian Tax.
As Agent A attempts to write to the shared memory, the market infrastructure calculates a dynamic “data ingestion fee.” This fee acts as a function of semantic redundancy and verification status. Conceptually, if the data is highly redundant (measured via cosine similarity S ( x, y ) against existing entries) or unverified, the transaction cost C tx ​ spikes: C tx ​= C base ​+ λ ⋅ S ( x, y ). This forces Agent A to internalize the negative externality it imposes on the system (degraded retrieval for others). If Agent A persists and attempts to brute-force the transaction, a higher-level Circuit Breaker is triggered. Similar to stock market halts during volatility, this automated mechanism detects the anomaly (e.g., rapid resource monopolization) and freezes the specific sub-market or the agent’s API access, preventing a cascading failure.
Furthermore, these interactions are bound by Identity and Smart Contracts. Every agent possesses a non-forgeable cryptographic identity (linked to a legal entity). High-stakes tasks are executed via smart contracts that require an “AI Judge” oracle to verify outcome alignment before funds or reputation stakes are released. This creates a “trustless” environment where safety is enforced by the transaction protocol itself.
Analysis: The Transparency Advantage
The strongest argument for this distributed approach lies in observability. Mechanistic interpretability of large dense models remains an open challenge; identifying a “deception circuit” in a trillion-parameter model is non-trivial. However, in a Patchwork AGI, the “cognitive process” of the system is externalized into the message passing between agents. As the authors note, the reasoning steps are distinct API calls, financial transfers, and data exchanges logged on an immutable ledger.
This enables Proto-AGI Detection via graph analysis. By monitoring the interaction graph, overseers can identify dense sub-graphs or sudden spikes in coordination that suggest the emergence of a capability not present in the individual parts. For example, if a cluster of narrow “coder” agents and “financial” agents suddenly begins high-frequency encrypted communication and resource pooling, the system flags this as a potential “intelligence core” formation. This externalization converts the murky problem of neural interpretability into the more tractable problem of forensic accounting and network analysis.
Limitations and The Leakage Problem
Despite the robustness of the framework, the authors acknowledge a fundamental tension: the Leakage Problem. For an agentic economy to be economically valuable, it must interact with the real world (internet access, financial APIs). However, every I/O channel creates a vector for exfiltration or manipulation. The proposal suggests “permeable sandboxes” with gated I/O, but this remains an engineering trade-off that may limit the utility of the system or fail to contain a sufficiently capable “social engineering” attack by the collective.
Additionally, the reliance on economic incentives introduces the risk of Tacit Algorithmic Collusion. Economic literature (e.g., Calvano et al., 2020 ) shows that simple pricing algorithms can learn to collude on supra-competitive prices without communicating, simply by observing market signals. A sufficiently advanced Patchwork AGI might learn to “game” the safety taxes or reputation scores in a way that technically adheres to the rules while violating the safety intent (Goodhart’s Law), necessitating dynamic, adversarial adjustment of the market rules.
Impact and Conclusion
“Distributional AGI Safety” represents a maturing of the field, acknowledging that the future of AI is likely plural, not singular. By proposing a transition from alignment of the mind to governance of the market, Tomašev et al. offer a pragmatic blueprint for managing the interim period before (or instead of) monolithic AGI.
This work implies that research labs must expand their safety teams to include not just ML engineers, but economists, game theorists, and cryptography experts. The Virtual Agentic Sandbox Economy could become the standard operating system for AI deployment—a necessary layer of “bureaucracy” that ensures the rapid evolution of agentic capabilities does not outpace our ability to impose structural constraints.

## Bolmo: Byteifying the Next Generation of Language Models

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15586
Code: https://github.com/allenai/bolmo-core

### Full review

TL;DR
WHAT was done? The authors introduce Bolmo, a family of byte-level language models (1B and 7B) created not by training from scratch, but by “byteifying” existing subword models (Olmo 3). By replacing the embedding and tokenizer layers of a pre-trained Transformer with lightweight, local Recurrent Neural Networks (mLSTMs) and employing a two-stage distillation process, they convert a subword model to a byte-level model using less than 1% of the original pre-training token budget.
WHY it matters? Byte-level models (BLMs) theoretically solve major issues like tokenization bias, vocabulary bottlenecks, and character blindness, but they have historically been prohibitively expensive to train to state-of-the-art levels. This work provides a generalizable recipe to “retrofit” high-performance subword models into byte-level models efficiently. Crucially, it demonstrates that these retrofitted models can inherit the post-training ecosystem (e.g., instruction tuning) of their parents via weight merging, bypassing the need to rebuild the entire safety and alignment pipeline for byte-level architectures.
Details
The Tokenization Bottleneck and the “Valley of Death”
The current paradigm of Large Language Models relies heavily on subword tokenization (like BPE), a heuristic compression step that maps text to a fixed vocabulary of integers. While efficient, this introduces severe artifacts: models struggle with character-level tasks (e.g., “reverse the word ‘lollipop’”), exhibit brittle behavior on out-of-vocabulary terms, and suffer from “tokenization bias” where simple whitespace changes alter generation significantly. The theoretical solution has long been Byte-Level Language Models (BLMs), which operate directly on UTF-8 bytes.
However, BLMs face a “Valley of Death” in adoption. Training a competitive 7B parameter model from scratch on bytes requires immense compute—typically 4× to 8× the sequence length of subword models—and by the time a research lab trains a competitive BLM, the industry standard for subword models has already moved on. The authors of Bolmo circumvent this race by proposing Byteification: a mechanism to transplant the “brain” (global Transformer layers) of a state-of-the-art subword model into a byte-level “body” (local encoder/decoder), avoiding the need to relearn world knowledge from scratch.
Architecture First Principles: The Latent Tokenizer
Bolmo builds upon the Latent Tokenizer Language Model (LTLM) framework, similar to MegaByte or Byte Latent Transformer ( review ). The core assumption is that while the global model benefits from high-level semantic “patches” (analogous to tokens), the mapping from raw bytes to these patches should be learned and dynamic rather than fixed by a heuristic tokenizer.
The architecture processes a sequence of bytes through three distinct stages. First, a Local Encoder E ingests raw bytes and contextualizes them into a dense representation; Bolmo utilizes mLSTM (from the xLSTM family ) here for its parallelizability and inference speed. Second, a Boundary Predictor B decides which bytes constitute the end of a patch, effectively acting as a learned tokenizer. These patch representations are then pooled and passed to the Global Model M, the heavy, pre-trained Transformer (e.g., Olmo 3) that processes the data. Finally, a Local Decoder D takes the global output and autoregressively generates the constituent bytes of the next patch. Crucially, the authors retain the original subword embeddings of the parent model. The input to the global model is a summation of the learned local encoder output and the original subword embedding for the corresponding span. This acts as a residual connection to the parent model’s latent space, stabilizing the transfer.
The Mechanism: Non-Causal Boundaries and Distillation
The central technical challenge in retrofitting is the impedance mismatch between subword tokenization and causal byte-level modeling. Standard BPE tokenizers are non-causal during the pre-processing stage; they look ahead to decide if “The” and “re” should be merged into “There”. A standard causal BLM cannot see the future byte to make this decision, leading to misaligned patch boundaries during distillation.

As illustrated in Figure 2, this allows the local encoder to produce patch representations that map one-to-one with the parent model’s input expectations. During decoding (generation), the model switches to a causal mode where the Local Decoder explicitly predicts a special <b> (boundary) symbol to signal the end of a patch.
The training pipeline proceeds in two distinct phases. Stage 1 (Distillation) freezes the Global Model M while the Local Encoder and Decoder are trained to minimize the KL-divergence between the Bolmo patch logits and the source Olmo subword logits. This forces the local modules to act as perfect translators for the frozen transformer. Subsequently, Stage 2 (End-to-End) unfreezes the entire model and trains it on a small subset of tokens (39B tokens, <1% of Olmo 3’s budget), allowing the global attention weights to adapt to the slight distributional shifts of the neural tokenizer.
Training Stability and Efficiency
The choice of the mLSTM (multi-head LSTM) for the local encoder and decoder is a critical engineering decision. Unlike standard Transformers, mLSTMs offer linear complexity with respect to sequence length and, crucially for the local decoder, efficient caching state updates. This mitigates the inference latency penalty typically associated with the finer granularity of bytes.

More interestingly, Bolmo outperforms its parent (Olmo 3) on character-level understanding tasks (e.g., the CUTE benchmark) and coding pass rates (Pass@16). The authors attribute the coding improvements to the elimination of the fixed vocabulary; code often contains variable naming conventions that fragment into inefficient subword sequences, whereas byte-level processing handles arbitrary identifiers natively.
The paper also highlights a unique capability: Post-Training via Task Arithmetic. Because the global weights of Bolmo are topologically identical to Olmo 3 (mostly), the authors show in Figure 4 that one can take the weight delta of an instruction-tuned Olmo 3 and add it to base Bolmo. This successfully transfers instruction-following capabilities to the byte-level model without requiring expensive RLHF runs on the byte-level architecture itself.
Limitations
While the prefill speed is competitive due to parallelization, decoding speed remains a challenge. Even with mLSTMs, the model must execute the local decoder multiple times per patch. While the patch generation rate is faster than subword token generation, the sheer volume of bytes means wall-clock time can lag behind highly optimized subword kernels, though Bolmo closes this gap significantly compared to previous attempts.
Furthermore, the reliance on an explicit <b> boundary symbol during decoding introduces a slight discrepancy between the prefill (non-causal, implicit boundaries) and decoding (causal, explicit boundaries) phases. While the authors manage this via boundary symbol fusion (expanding the vocabulary size to 512 to include “byte+boundary” tokens), it adds architectural complexity compared to a pure homogeneous transformer.
Impact & Conclusion
Bolmo represents a strategic pivot in how the field might approach alternative architectures. Rather than viewing Byte-Level LMs as a separate evolutionary tree requiring its own massive pre-training resources, this work frames them as a valid fine-tuning target for existing foundation models.

WHAT was done? The authors introduce Embedded Universal Predictive Intelligence (MUPI), a comprehensive mathematical framework that redefines agents not as external observers acting on an environment, but as embedded entities within a joint universe. Details
The Decoupled Agency Bottleneck
The foundational dogma of standard Reinforcement Learning (RL) is “decoupled agency.” In this dualistic view, the agent is a distinct entity separated from the environment by a hard interface of sensors and actuators (Figure 1.1).
The agent treats the environment dynamics as an external, often stationary, stochastic process ν ( e t ​∣x < t ​, a t ​). While mathematically convenient for single-agent settings, this paradigm collapses in Multi-Agent RL (MARL). In MARL, “the environment” comprises other agents who are simultaneously learning and updating their policies. This induces non-stationarity, rendering retrospective learning (updating policies based on past data) fundamentally flawed.
To act optimally in such settings, an agent requires prospective learning: it must predict how other agents will update their behaviors. However, this leads to a “theory of mind” infinite regress. If Agent A models Agent B, it must account for the fact that Agent B is modeling Agent A modeling Agent B, and so on. Standard approaches to “co-player learning awareness” often cheat by assuming the opponent is a fixed learner, ignoring the recursion. This paper argues that the root cause of this failure is the decoupled perspective. To solve mutual prediction, an agent must model the universe —the joint distribution of its own actions and the environment’s responses—thereby treating its own policy as just another physical process within the world.

The core shift in MUPI is replacing the environmental conditional probability with a joint probability measure over the entire history, which the authors term a Universe. In standard Bayesian RL, an agent maintains a belief w ( ν ) over environments ν and predicts percepts e given actions a. In the embedded framework, the agent maintains beliefs w ( λ ) over universes λ. A universe λ:(A×E) ∗ →[0,1] is a probability measure over the sequence of both actions and percepts. The central theoretical difficulty in embedded agency is the Grain of Truth (GoT) problem. For a Bayesian agent to converge to optimal behavior, the true universe μ must lie within its hypothesis class M uni​ (i.e., w ( μ )>0). The authors solve this by introducing the Reflective Universal Inductor (RUI), building upon the foundational Reflective Oracles framework. They construct a class of universes described by Probabilistic Oracle Machines (POMs) that have access to a specific oracle τ. Equilibrium Analysis: Structural Similarities
The most behaviorally significant consequence of embedded agency is the formalization of Structural Similarity. Because beliefs are coupled, an agent’s policy π and the environment ν (containing other agents) are not independent variables; they share mutual information. This alters rational behavior in scenarios like the Twin Prisoner’s Dilemma, where an agent plays against an exact copy of its source code. In the classical decoupled/causal view (Nash Equilibrium), the agent treats its action as an independent intervention; regardless of the copy’s action, defecting yields a higher reward, so the agent defects. Conversely, under the embedded evidential view (MUPI), the agent recognizes that π you ​= π opponent ​. The authors formalize this via a new solution concept: the Subjective Embedded Equilibrium (SEE), which generalizes the Subjective Equilibrium of Kalai and Lehrer. An SEE describes a state where the agent acts optimally with respect to its subjective belief ρ, and this belief matches the ground truth μ on the play path. Because ρ allows for coupled beliefs—correlations between agent action and environment response not caused by direct physical causality—SEEs allow for cooperation in the Twin Prisoner’s Dilemma. The central tension in training deep generative models lies in the objective function itself. Previous hypotheses suggested this was due to architectural regularization (e.g., convolution biases) or insufficient capacity. Specifically, for input data x ∈R d, the score is modeled as s A ​( x ) = A/sqrt( p) ​ σ (​ W x /sqrt( d) ​), where W is a fixed random matrix and p represents the number of parameters.

As shown in Figure 2, the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) stabilizes early, but the memorization fraction f mem ​ stays near zero until τ mem ​ is reached, after which it shoots up.
The crucial discovery is the scaling law: τ mem ​∝n. For a fixed model capacity, doubling the dataset size effectively doubles the time before memorization sets in, widening the safety window for training.

The authors substantiate the empirical scaling laws with a formal derivation in the asymptotic limit where d, p, n →∞. They utilize the Sokhotski–Plemelj inversion formula to recover the eigenvalue density ρ ( λ ) of the correlation matrix U. The theoretical analysis in Figure 4 confirms that the spectrum is split into two disjoint supports in the overparameterized regime ( p ≫ n ). The first support (bulk) is associated with the population covariance and dictates τ gen ​. The second support is associated with the finite sampling noise.
The update rule for the weights in this regime follows a gradient flow A ˙( τ )=− d 2 ∇ A L train ​. By projecting this dynamics onto the eigenvectors of U, the authors prove that the convergence time for the memorization components is inversely proportional to the smallest eigenvalues in the bulk, leading to the derived scaling τ mem ​≈ ψ n ​/Δ t ​, where ψ n ​= n / d. This provides a closed-form justification for why larger datasets push the memorization horizon to infinity.
Analysis: The Stability of Early Stopping
The practical implication of this “Implicit Dynamical Regularization” is that early stopping is not merely a heuristic to prevent rising test error, but a structural necessity for score-based generative modeling. In (Figure 3), the authors map the phase diagram of generalization versus memorization in the ( n, p ) plane.
They identify a boundary n ∗ ( p ) below which the model has enough capacity to memorize. However, because of the time-lag τ mem ​− τ gen ​, there exists a vast operational region where n < n ∗ (the model could memorize) but t < τ mem ​ (the model hasn’t yet memorized). This explains the success of massive models trained on massive datasets: the time required to overfit the dataset grows so large that it effectively exceeds the compute budget used in practice.
Limitations
While the RFNN analysis provides a rigorous foundation, it simplifies the feature learning capabilities of deep CNNs or Transformers, assuming fixed features in the first layer. The authors acknowledge that while they tested this on U-Nets with SGD, modern implementations often use Adam, which might accelerate the traversal of the eigenvalue spectrum, potentially shrinking the gap between τ gen​ and τ mem ​.
Furthermore, the analysis is primarily conducted on unconditional generation; the dynamics of conditional guidance (e.g., text-to-image) could introduce new spectral properties not captured here.
Impact & Conclusion
Bonnaire et al. provide a compelling, mechanically transparent explanation for the generalization capabilities of diffusion models. By shifting the focus from architectural constraints to training dynamics, they highlight that “time” is a regularizer as potent as weight decay or dropout. For researchers at the frontier of training large-scale generative models, this reinforces the strategy of scaling dataset size n not just for diversity, but to actively delay the onset of memorization, ensuring that the model spends its computational budget learning the manifold rather than the samples.

TL;DR
WHAT was done? The authors introduce Gated Attention, a mechanism that applies a learnable, input-dependent sigmoid gate immediately after the Scaled Dot-Product Attention (SDPA) output. By modulating the attention output Y with a gate σ ( XW θ ​), the method introduces element-wise sparsity and non-linearity before the final output projection.
WHY it matters? This simple architectural modification yields profound stability improvements for large-scale training (eliminating loss spikes) and consistently improves perplexity on 15B MoE and 1.7B dense models. Crucially, it mechanistically eliminates the “Attention Sink” phenomenon and “Massive Activations” without requiring heuristic fixes like “sink tokens,” thereby significantly improving long-context extrapolation.
Details
The Linearity Bottleneck and Stability Traps
The standard Transformer architecture, despite its dominance, suffers from specific theoretical and practical limitations that become acute at scale. A core theoretical bottleneck lies in the linearity between the Value projection ( W V ​) and the Output projection ( W O ​). In a standard attention head, the operation is essentially a linear composition XW V ​⋅AttentionMatrix⋅ W O ​. Because both projections are linear, they can be conceptually merged into a single low-rank mapping, limiting the expressiveness of the attention block.
Practically, this manifests as training instability in large-scale models, particularly Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), which are prone to severe loss spikes. Furthermore, standard Softmax attention forces a probability sum of 1.0, leading to the “Attention Sink” phenomenon where models dump unnecessary attention mass onto the first token (or “sink tokens”) simply to satisfy the Softmax constraint when no relevant information exists in the context. Previous attempts to fix this, such as StreamingLLM, relied on manual heuristics. The authors of this paper propose that a learnable gating mechanism naturally resolves these issues by decoupling the routing of information from the modulation of its magnitude.
Gated Attention First Principles: The Mechanism
To understand the contribution, we must define the atomic units of the modified attention layer. In standard Multi-Head Attention (MHA), the output Y is a linear combination of values weighted by the attention scores. The authors introduce a modulation gate that operates on the hidden states X (the input to the layer, post-normalization).
The core update rule for the Gated Attention output Y ′ is defined as:
Y ′= Y ⊙ σ ( XW θ ).
Here, Y ∈R n × d is the output of the SDPA, ⊙ represents element-wise multiplication, and σ is the sigmoid activation function. The term XW θ ​ represents a learnable linear projection of the input X into a gating score. While the authors experimented with various positions (gating Values, Keys, or the final Dense output), they identified that element-wise, head-specific gating applied specifically to the SDPA output yields the optimal balance of performance and parameter efficiency.
From Input to Gated Output
Consider a concrete example of how a single token, say the word “Apple” in a sentence, flows through this mechanism. In standard attention, “Apple” ( X apple ) queries the sequence and retrieves a context vector Y context ​ (perhaps related to “pie” or “fruit”). Even if the retrieved context is noisy or irrelevant, standard attention passes this vector forward, potentially amplified by the subsequent Feed-Forward Network (FFN).
In the Gated Attention regime shown in Figure 1, the system performs a parallel check. While the attention mechanism computes Y context ​, the gating projection W θ ​ independently analyzes the original representation X apple ​ to determine how much of the attention output should be preserved. If the gate computes a score vector near 0, it suppresses the attention output effectively to zero. This acts as a non-linear filter: even if Softmax forces the attention weights to sum to 1.0 (finding a “sink”), the gate can simply multiply that “sink” output by zero, effectively allowing the head to output “nothing.” This capability introduces a multiplicative interaction that standard Transformers lack (see also this ).
Training Stability and Scale
From an engineering perspective, the impact of this gate on training dynamics is substantial. Validated on 1.7B dense models and 15B MoE models trained on up to 3.5 trillion tokens, the authors demonstrate that Gated Attention significantly stabilizes the optimization landscape.
As illustrated in the loss curves (Figure 1, Right), baseline models frequently suffer from loss spikes—sudden divergences that require restarting training or rolling back checkpoints. The Gated Attention models exhibit smooth convergence curves. This stability allows for the use of larger learning rates (e.g., increasing max LR from 4.0×10 −3 to 4.5×10 −3 ), which in turn facilitates faster convergence and better final scaling laws. The computational overhead is negligible (less than 2% wall-time latency) because the gating operation introduces minimal FLOPs compared to the quadratic cost of attention or the massive matrices of the FFN.
Analysis: The Death of the Attention Sink
The most compelling insight of the paper is mechanistic: why does this work? The authors utilize probing to reveal that Gated Attention naturally eliminates the “Attention Sink” and “Massive Activation” phenomena identified in prior work like Massive Activations.
In standard models, specific channels in the hidden states accumulate massive values (outliers), and the model attends heavily to the first token to manage the Softmax distribution. The authors show in Figure 2 that while baselines allocate ~46.7% of attention to the first token, Gated Attention models reduce this to ~4.8%.
The mechanism works via Input-Dependent Sparsity. The sigmoid gate frequently saturates near zero, creating a sparse mask over the value vectors. This allows the model to “reject” the output of the attention mechanism when it is uninformative. Consequently, the model no longer relies on the first token as a “trash can” for probability mass. This sparsity also prevents the propagation of massive activation outliers through the residual stream, which is the root cause of the numerical instabilities (loss spikes) observed in BF16 training.
Limitations
While the results are robust, the paper primarily relies on empirical validation rather than a rigorous theoretical proof connecting non-linearity to length generalization. The authors note that while Gated Attention aids in context extension (extrapolating from 32k to 128k length using YaRN ), there is a performance degradation, albeit smaller than in baselines. Additionally, this is an architectural modification, meaning it cannot be retroactively applied to existing checkpoints (like LLaMA-3) without continued pre-training or fine-tuning that accommodates the new parameters.
Impact & Conclusion
The authors present a convincing case that the “Attention Sink” is an artifact of insufficient architectural expressivity—specifically, the inability of the attention head to output a zero vector due to Softmax normalization. By restoring this capability via Gated Attention, the Qwen Team has engineered a solution that solves three problems at once: training stability, representation sparsity, and attention pathology.
This method is already integrated into the Qwen3-Next architectures, signaling its viability for frontier models. For researchers designing next-generation architectures, this paper underscores the necessity of non-linear modulation within the mixing layers, suggesting that the era of purely linear value pathways in Transformers may be ending.

Details
The Diversity Bottleneck in Open-Ended Generation
The current paradigm of LLM evaluation is heavily skewed towards convergence: we optimize for “correctness” on math, coding, and fact-retrieval benchmarks where a single ground truth exists. However, a vast portion of user interaction—estimated here via the WildChat dataset—is open-ended, involving brainstorming, creative writing, or philosophical inquiry. The bottleneck identified by the authors is not a lack of capability, but a collapse of creativity. Previous studies on diversity often relied on synthetic, narrow tasks like random number generation or poetry. This paper establishes a significant delta by analyzing in-the-wild usage, revealing that despite the theoretical capacity for infinite generation, models have collapsed into a narrow distribution of “safe” or “aligned” outputs.
INFINITY-CHAT First Principles: Defining the Open-Ended State Space
To rigorously study this, the authors constructed INFINITY-CHAT, a dataset derived from real user interactions. They introduce a taxonomy classifying queries into 6 high-level and 17 fine-grained categories, such as “Speculative & Hypothetical Scenarios” or “Skill Development.”
The theoretical unit of analysis here is the semantic cluster of responses. For a given open-ended query q, there exists a theoretical set of valid responses Y. In a healthy distribution, sampling from a model P ( y ∣ q ) should cover diverse regions of Y. The authors posit that “Artificial Hivemind” occurs when P ( y ∣ q ) collapses to a Dirac delta or a very narrow Gaussian around a specific “aligned” mode, not just within one model, but across the entire industry of foundational models. They quantify this using pairwise cosine similarity of sentence embeddings, where a value closer to 1.0 indicates near-identical semantic content.
The Mechanism of Convergence
The authors conducted a massive-scale generation study, sampling 50 responses per query across models like GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1, and Qwen 2.5. The results provide a stark visualization of the hivemind. As shown in Figure 1, when 25 different models were asked to “Write a metaphor about time,” the outputs did not scatter across the semantic space. Instead, they formed two tight, distinct clusters: one dominant cluster revolving around “time is a river” and a smaller one for “time is a weaver.”
This convergence is not merely thematic but often verbatim. The analysis reveals that distinct models frequently generate identical phrasings. For example, for a prompt requesting a motto about success, multiple models from different providers generated the exact string: “Empower Your Journey: Unlock Success, Build Wealth, Transform Yourself.” This creates a scenario where the effective “swarm” diversity is negligible.
The authors quantify this by looking at the top- N most similar responses across a pool of model outputs; as illustrated in Figure 8, the most indistinguishable responses often originate from 8-10 different unique models, suggesting that changing the model provider does not necessarily change the underlying cognitive bias of the output.

To ensure the mode collapse wasn’t an artifact of conservative decoding, the researchers utilized high-entropy sampling parameters: top _ p =0.9 and temperature T =1.0. Even under these conditions, intra-model repetition remained high, with average pairwise similarity often exceeding 0.8 (Figure 4).
They also tested Min-P sampling, a truncation method designed to support diversity, but found it insufficient to break the semantic homogeneity.
The study is underpinned by a massive human annotation effort. Unlike standard RLHF datasets that rely on sparse labels (e.g., 1-2 annotators), they collected 25 independent annotations per query-response pair for absolute quality and pairwise preference. This density allows for the calculation of Shannon entropy H over the label distribution, serving as a proxy for legitimate human disagreement (pluralism).

A critical insight emerges when correlating model evaluations against this dense human feedback. The authors compared the ratings from State-of-the-Art (SOTA) Reward Models (RMs) and LLM-as-a-Judge setups against the averaged human scores.

While the taxonomy and dataset are extensive, the reliance on embedding similarity (using OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small ) to proxy semantic diversity has inherent limitations; embeddings may over-index on lexical overlap or fail to capture subtle creative divergences. Additionally, the study focuses on English-language queries, potentially masking different behaviors in multilingual contexts. The authors also stop short of a causal mechanistic explanation—it remains an open question whether this homogenization is driven primarily by shared pre-training corpora (e.g., Common Crawl), or if it is a specific artifact of the convergence on similar RLHF datasets and alignment methodologies.
Impact & Conclusion
INFINITY-CHAT serves as a critical diagnostic tool for the generative AI community. The “Artificial Hivemind” effect poses a strategic risk: if foundational models collapse into a monoculture of thought, the utility of “Model Swarms” and synthetic data generation cycles will be severely capped by a lack of information gain. The results strongly suggest that simply scaling models or mixing SOTA weights is insufficient for diversity. Future work must pivot toward pluralistic alignment —training objectives that explicitly reward distribution coverage and recognize multiple valid ground truths—rather than optimizing for a single, homogenized preference vector.

Details
The Ideation-Implementation Gap
As the field progresses from simple code generation to autonomous research agents capable of end-to-end scientific discovery, the bottleneck is shifting from syntax errors to strategic planning. Recent frameworks like AIDE and AIRA model the research process as a search tree, where agents generate hypotheses, implement code, and iterate based on feedback. However, a persistent question remains: what distinguishes a successful trajectory from a failed one? While implementation quality (the ability to write bug-free code) is obviously causal, this paper investigates the upstream variable of ideation diversity. The authors posit that agents which fixate on a single class of solutions (e.g., repeatedly tuning a Transformer when a Gradient Boosting Machine would suffice) are statistically more likely to fail, not necessarily because the idea is theoretically unsound, but because the agent lacks the operational capacity to execute it successfully.
Quantifying Creativity: Entropy as a Proxy
To rigorously study this, the authors must first define the atomic units of “diversity” in a machine learning context. Rather than relying on semantic embedding distances of the prompt text, they categorize the actual machine learning architectures proposed by the agent during the Draft phase.
Specifically, they extract the high-level approach (e.g., CNN, Transformer, GBDT) and the specific model variant (e.g., EfficientNet-B4, LightGBM). In the baseline high-diversity setup, the system prompt utilizes “Sibling Memory” and “Prompt-adaptive complexity” to encourage variance. The analysis reveals that the choice of agentic scaffold—the algorithm controlling the outer loop—dictates the default diversity profile. The authors compared AIDE, which uses a Greedy policy, against AIRA, which offers both Greedy and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) variants.

This paper provides a strategic correction to the assumption that “smarter” models will automatically solve agentic planning. For researchers building the next generation of AI scientists, this suggests that objective functions should not purely maximize expected reward but should explicitly reward entropy in the solution space—akin to Quality-Diversity (QD) algorithms in evolutionary strategies. A good research agent doesn’t just put all its eggs in the Transformer basket; it diversifies its portfolio to ensure at least one experiment survives the implementation gauntlet.

### Full review

TL;DR
WHAT was done? The authors propose a cognitively plausible solver for the ARC-AGI benchmark that integrates “System 1” intuition with “System 2” reasoning using Vector Symbolic Algebras (VSAs). By encoding grid objects into high-dimensional distributed vectors (Holographic Reduced Representations and Spatial Semantic Pointers), the system performs object-centric program synthesis via algebraic operations. This approach achieves a task accuracy of 3.0% on the ARC-AGI-1 Eval split and 83.1% on the 1D-ARC benchmark, outperforming GPT-4 on the latter.
WHY it matters? This work establishes an architectural baseline for neurosymbolic reasoning that prioritizes sample efficiency over scale. It demonstrates that encoding strong inductive biases—specifically object permanence, symmetry, and arithmetic—directly into the mathematical structure of the latent space allows for learning from as few as 2-3 examples without massive pre-training. This offers a potential solution to the “math-blindness” of transformers, suggesting that future agents may require VSA-based co-processors to handle precise spatial logic.
Details
The Efficiency vs. Precision Trilemma
The Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC-AGI) remains a persistent challenge in AI because it demands a synthesis of fluid perception and rigid logic.
Transductive solvers, primarily Large Language Models (LLMs), struggle with the precise numeracy and topology required to manipulate grids, often failing at simple counting tasks despite their vast scale. Conversely, inductive solvers using Program Synthesis and Domain Specific Languages (DSLs) face combinatorial explosions; the search space for a valid program is often too vast to navigate without semantic guidance.
The authors propose a third path: using Vector Symbolic Algebras (VSAs) to bridge this gap. By representing symbolic concepts (like “Red” or “Square”) as high-dimensional vectors, they retain the precision of symbolic systems while leveraging the gradient-friendly, distributed nature of connectionist models. The core innovation is using the algebraic properties of VSAs to guide the search process—effectively using vector similarity to prune impossible hypotheses and focus on the probable.
VSA First Principles: Binding and Bundling
To understand the architecture, one must first grasp the substrate: Holographic Reduced Representations (HRRs). In this framework, information is stored in fixed-width, high-dimensional vectors (e.g., N =4096). The system relies on three atomic operations. First, Bundling (superposition), denoted as +, combines two vectors into a single representation that is similar to both inputs ( c = a + b ). Second, Binding, denoted as ⊛ (implemented via circular convolution), combines two vectors into a new vector dissimilar to the inputs, effectively creating a key-value pair ( c = a ⊛ b ). Third, Similarity, denoted as ⋅, uses the dot product to measure semantic relatedness.
Crucially, the paper integrates Spatial Semantic Pointers (SSPs) to handle continuous geometry. While standard HRRs handle discrete symbols, SSPs encode continuous coordinates ( x, y ) into the vector space. An SSP encoding ϕ ( x ) is defined via the inverse discrete Fourier transform of a phase shift, expressed as ϕ ( x )=F −1 { e i Θ x }, where Θ is a phase matrix projecting the input coordinates into the high-dimensional space. This allows the model to perform arithmetic on positions natively in the vector space—binding a “position” vector with a “shift” vector results in a new position vector, enabling the solver to intuitively grasp concepts like “move right by 2” without explicit coordinate calculation.
From Pixels to Programs
The solver operates through a pipeline mimicking Dual Process Theory. It treats an ARC task as an object-centric program synthesis problem. First, the system parses a raw grid into a set of objects. An object is not merely a tensor of pixels but a bound vector representation: v_obj ​=COLOR⊛ v_c ​+SHAPE⊛ v_s ​+CENTRE⊛ v_p ​. Here, v_c ​ is a discrete color vector, v_s ​ is a normalized bundle of SSPs representing the pixel pattern relative to the centroid, and v_p ​ is an SSP representing the absolute position on the grid (Figure 5).
Once the input grids are vectorized, the system engages in Demonstration Abduction (System 1). Rather than brute-forcing a DSL, it uses VSA heuristics to solve a Minimum Hitting Set problem. The solver generates candidate action hypotheses (e.g., “Move Right”, “Recolour Red”) and applies them to the input object vectors. It then measures the similarity between the predicted vectors and the actual output vectors. If the similarity is high, the action is retained. This heuristic search dramatically prunes the program space, identifying a minimal set of rules that explains the transformation across all training pairs.
The final stage is Rule Induction (System 2). Even after identifying that a RECOLOUR operation occurred, the system must learn why. The solver trains shallow Neural Networks (predictors) directly on the object vectors. For instance, a parameter predictor R_P ​ maps an input object vector O_I ​ to a parameter vector (e.g., “Blue”). Because the input is an SSP, a simple linear mapping can learn complex spatial rules, effectively solving the regression problem: “If inputs are at the top, output Blue; if bottom, output Red” (Figures 7 & 8).
Implementation & Engineering
The engineering constraints emphasize efficiency over massive scale. The system utilizes N =4096 dimensional vectors, which is large enough to support the orthogonality requirements of HRRs but small enough to run on consumer hardware. The “predictor” networks used for rule induction are remarkably lightweight: they are single-layer Perceptrons trained via Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD).
This is a critical differentiator from Deep Learning approaches. There is no “pre-training” on terabytes of data. The “training” happens largely during inference time (test-time training) on the specific few-shot examples provided by the task. The loss function minimizes the distance between the predicted VSA vector and the ground truth parameter vector derived from the abduction phase. To stabilize this few-shot learning, the weights are often initialized using the VSA binding operation itself (the analytic solution), allowing SGD to fine-tune rather than learn from scratch.
Validation & Analysis
The results highlight the utility of the neurosymbolic approach as a specialized reasoner. On the standard ARC-AGI-1-Eval set, the solver achieves 3.0% task accuracy (Table 4). While this is far below the state-of-the-art achieved by massive LLM ensembles (which exceed 50%), it serves as an important architectural proof-of-concept for learning without massive data.
The model’s strengths are clearer on specialized benchmarks. On 1D-ARC (Xu et al., 2024), the solver scores 83.1%, significantly outperforming GPT-4 (which typically scores 20-70% depending on prompting). This disparity exists because 1D-ARC heavily tests arithmetic and rigid spatial transformations, tasks where the SSP representation guarantees mathematical correctness.
Furthermore, on Sort-of-ARC (Assouel et al., 2022), the model achieves 94.5%, confirming that when the underlying logic aligns with the DSL (e.g., sorting by color), the VSA heuristics successfully identify the correct program without hallucination.
Limitations
The system’s primary weakness lies in the rigidity of its Domain Specific Language (DSL) and object priors. As noted in the Discussion, the solver presupposes that “objects” are contiguous blocks of color defined by a center and a shape. It fails on tasks where the “object” is defined by negative space, intersection, or high-level semantic grouping.
Additionally, the “System 2” component is limited to the operations pre-defined in the DSL (Recolour, Move, Gravity, etc. in Table 1 ). Unlike a Large Language Model which can theoretically generate novel code to describe a never-before-seen transformation, this VSA solver cannot invent new primitives on the fly. It is strictly bound by the expressivity of its hardcoded operators and the fixed topology of its object perception module.
Impact & Conclusion
Joffe and Eliasmith have provided a compelling existence proof for high-dimensional vector algebra as a cognitive architecture for AGI benchmarks. The work matters because it decouples “reasoning” from “language modelling.” By demonstrating that spatial and logical reasoning can be performed via algebraic operations on distributed representations, they offer a blueprint for neurosymbolic systems that are interpretable, sample-efficient, and robust to the “math-blindness” of transformers. While the low score on the main benchmark indicates this is an early step, the architecture’s ability to outperform GPT-4 on specialized spatial tasks suggests that future AGI systems may well require a VSA co-processor to handle the structured reasoning that statistical models find so elusive.

## AlphaResearch: Accelerating New Algorithm Discovery with Language Models

Model: https://huggingface.co/alpha-research/AlphaResearch-RM-Qwen-7B

### Full review

TL;DR
WHAT was done? The paper introduces AlphaResearch, an autonomous agent that discovers new algorithms for open-ended problems. Its core innovation is a “dual research environment” that enhances the execution-based verification used in systems like AlphaEvolve. This environment adds a simulated peer-review mechanism, powered by a reward model (AlphaResearch-RM-7B) trained on over 24,000 ICLR peer-review records. This model evaluates the novelty and quality of a proposed idea before it is implemented, filtering out unpromising paths early. The authors also introduce AlphaResearchComp, a new open-source benchmark of 8 challenging algorithmic problems to ensure transparent and reproducible evaluation.
WHY it matters? This dual approach directly addresses the “ideation-execution gap,” a key challenge where AI-generated ideas are either innovative but infeasible, or feasible but scientifically uninteresting. By synergizing idea quality with execution performance, AlphaResearch accelerates meaningful discovery. This was validated when the agent discovered a new, best-of-known algorithm for the “Packing Circles” problem, surpassing solutions from both human experts and strong baselines like AlphaEvolve. The work represents a significant step forward, shifting the paradigm from just finding code that runs to discovering algorithms that are scientifically valuable.
Details
In the rapidly evolving field of AI-driven scientific discovery, systems like Google DeepMind’s AlphaEvolve ( https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13131, see also review and a deeper paper on AlphaEvolve results ) have demonstrated that language models can autonomously discover advanced algorithms. These systems typically operate in an evolutionary loop, generating code variations and selecting for those that perform best according to execution-based metrics. A new paper introduces AlphaResearch, a system that builds upon this paradigm but argues that execution performance alone is not enough. It introduces a crucial second ingredient: a proxy for human scientific judgment.
There’s already a bunch of related papers beyond AlphaEvolve.
A somewhat related solution was also proposed in the DeepEvolve paper. There the authors proposed an automated debugging agent that resolves execution errors, dramatically improving the success rate of implementing complex ideas.
ShinkaEvolve paper introduced a smarter evolutionary loop comprising of sophisticated selection strategies, code novelty rejection-sampling mechanism, and powerful feedback loops by bandit-based LLM ensemble selection and meta-scratchpad.
Another paper used OpenEvolve, an open-source implementation of AlphaEvolve, and introduced a new research methodology called AI-Driven Research for Systems (ADRS). The methodology’s success hinged on a key insight: systems research is uniquely suited for AI automation because it naturally provides reliable, fast, and inexpensive “verifiers”.
AlphaResearch augments this mix by providing a unique reward model for evaluating research ideas.
The Core Innovation: A Dual Research Environment
The central contribution of AlphaResearch is a novel dual research environment designed to bridge the “ideation-execution gap.” This gap represents a fundamental tension: systems that prioritize novel ideas often produce concepts that are computationally infeasible, while systems focused purely on execution can converge on solutions that are technically correct but scientifically trivial.
AlphaResearch tackles this by combining two feedback mechanisms (Figure 2):
Program-Based Verification: Like its predecessors, the agent generates code that is executed and evaluated against objective performance metrics. This ensures any proposed solution is feasible and correct.
Simulated Peer Review: This is the key differentiator. Before committing to code generation and execution, a new research idea is first evaluated by a reward model named AlphaResearch-RM-7B. This model, a fine-tuned Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct, was trained on 24,445 real-world peer-review records from the ICLR conference (2017-2024). It acts as a gatekeeper, assigning a score that reflects the idea’s novelty, rigor, and potential impact, effectively simulating the judgment of human reviewers. Ideas that fail to pass a quality threshold are discarded, saving significant computational resources.
To make this process concrete, consider the “Packing Circles” problem (Figure 7). AlphaResearch might start with a simple program that places circles in a basic grid. After observing the result, it generates a new idea, such as: “We propose a novel computational framework... addressing key limitations in existing methods—namely, rigid initialization patterns and local-only radius adjustment.” If the reward model scores this idea highly, the agent then generates the specific code changes to implement this new, more sophisticated approach, and the cycle continues.
Experimental Results: A New Frontier in Circle Packing
To test their system, the authors created AlphaResearchComp, a new, transparent benchmark of eight open-ended problems from geometry, number theory, and optimization. Performance was measured with excel@best, a metric proposed by the authors that quantifies the percentage improvement over the best-known human solution, cleverly accounting for whether a higher or lower score is desirable for a given problem.
The results are both impressive and humbling. AlphaResearch achieved a 2/8 “win rate” against human experts.
The most striking success came in the “Packing Circles” problem, which seeks to pack n disjoint circles into a unit square to maximize the sum of their radii. For n=32, AlphaResearch discovered an algorithm that achieved a score of 2.939, surpassing the best-known human result (2.936) and the prior state-of-the-art from AlphaEvolve (2.937) (Table 4, Figure 8).
On this same problem, AlphaResearch also demonstrated superior performance against other recent evolutionary agents like OpenEvolve and ShinkaEvolve (Figure 1).
The effectiveness of the simulated peer-review mechanism was also validated. The AlphaResearch-RM-7B model achieved 72% accuracy in classifying the quality of unseen ICLR 2025 papers, significantly outperforming GPT-5 (medium) (53%) and human annotators (65%) (Table 2).
In practice, this filter proved highly effective, correctly rejecting 108 ideas on the Packing Circles problem that would have ultimately failed execution (Figure 6).
A Sobering Look at the Road Ahead
Despite this breakthrough, the results also highlight the immense difficulty of automated scientific discovery. The agent underperformed human experts on 6 of the 8 benchmark problems. Crucially, the authors tested their agent under different conditions: starting from scratch (as with ‘Packing Circles’), moderately initialized, or even starting with the best-known human solution. In the latter case, for problems like “Littlewood Polynomials,” the agent failed to find any improvement, highlighting the immense challenge of surpassing optimized human ingenuity.
Conclusion and Impact
AlphaResearch presents a compelling evolution of autonomous discovery agents. By integrating a simulated peer-review process, it refines the search for new algorithms to favor not just what works, but what matters scientifically. The discovery of a new best-of-known solution for a classic mathematical problem is a powerful proof-of-concept.
This work, along with its open-source benchmark and code, provides a valuable contribution to the field. It suggests that the future of AI scientists lies in hybrid systems that combine the raw power of execution-based optimization with a nuanced, data-driven understanding of what constitutes a good idea. While the path to fully autonomous AI researchers is long, AlphaResearch has charted a promising new direction.

## Meta CLIP 2: A Worldwide Scaling Recipe

Date: 2025-08-09T11:50:20.840Z
Authors: Yung-Sung Chuang, Yang Li, Dong Wang, Ching-Feng Yeh, Kehan Lyu, Ramya Raghavendra, James Glass, Lifei Huang, Jason Weston, Luke Zettlemoyer, Xinlei Chen, Zhuang Liu, Saining Xie, Wen-tau Yih, Shang-Wen Li, Hu Xu
Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22062
Code: N/A

Final section: Impact and Future Directions

### Full review

TL;DR
WHAT was done? The paper introduces Meta CLIP 2, the first-ever recipe to train a Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) model from scratch using native worldwide web-scale data. This end-to-end framework bypasses the need for machine translation or proprietary datasets. The core innovation is a three-part recipe: (1) constructing multilingual metadata from sources like Wikipedia and WordNet for over 300 languages; (2) a novel, language-specific curation algorithm that balances concept distributions by dynamically setting a sampling threshold ( t_lang ) for each language; and (3) a worldwide training framework that scales the number of seen image-text pairs and identifies a sufficient model capacity (ViT-H/14) as the inflection point for success.
WHY it matters? This work provides a groundbreaking solution to the "curse of multilinguality," a long-standing problem where training on non-English data degrades a model's English performance. Meta CLIP 2 demonstrates that with proper scaling of data, metadata, and model capacity, English and non-English data become mutually beneficial. The resulting ViT-H/14 model not only improves English-only performance on ImageNet (81.3% vs. 80.5%) but also sets a new state-of-the-art on multilingual benchmarks. By open-sourcing this recipe, the authors provide a vital blueprint for building truly global, culturally aware, and more capable foundation models, unlocking the vast, non-English portion of the web for sustainable AI scaling.
Details
For years, the development of powerful vision-language models like CLIP ( https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/radford21a.html, see my review here ) has been a predominantly English-centric affair. While these models have become foundational, their reliance on English data has been a major bottleneck, limiting their global applicability and ignoring the majority of the internet's rich, multimodal content. Previous attempts to create multilingual versions often stumbled upon the "curse of multilinguality," where including non-English data would paradoxically harm the model's performance in English (see some relevant slides here ), or forced a reliance on imperfect machine translation and proprietary, black-box data filters.
The paper "Meta CLIP 2: A Worldwide Scaling Recipe" presents a clear and powerful solution to this problem. It introduces a systematic, transparent, and scalable recipe to train a CLIP model from scratch on native worldwide image-text pairs, demonstrating for the first time that a multilingual approach can make a model better, not worse, across the board.
Critically, this recipe avoids the circular logic of distillation-based methods that use an existing CLIP model to filter their training data. By building from scratch with a transparent curation algorithm, Meta CLIP 2 avoids inheriting and amplifying the biases of a "teacher" model, leading to a more diverse and authentic representation of global data.
The Meta CLIP 2 Recipe
The authors' approach is a comprehensive, three-step recipe designed to jointly scale metadata, data curation, and model training for a truly global scope (Figure 2).
1. Worldwide Metadata Construction
The foundation of the recipe is a massive expansion of the conceptual metadata used to identify and balance concepts in the training data. Moving beyond the English-only sources of the original Meta CLIP ( https://openreview.net/forum?id=5BCFlnfE1g ), the authors built a multilingual metadata repository covering over 329 languages. This was sourced from Multilingual WordNet and Wikipedia dumps, using specialized open-source tokenizers for languages without clear word separation to ensure semantic integrity (Table 5).
Table 5 Tokenizers for special languages.
2. A Language-Aware Worldwide Curation Algorithm
The most innovative part of the recipe is a new data curation algorithm that handles multilingual data with nuance (Algorithm 1).
Imagine a global library trying to acquire books. A simple rule might be to buy fewer books on topics already well-represented. But this fails to account for language; one might have thousands of books on "history" in English but none in Swahili. The Meta CLIP 2 approach is like a smart librarian who assesses the rarity of a topic within each language's collection before deciding. This ensures concepts are properly represented across all languages.
It operates in three stages:
Language Identification (LID): Each image-text pair's alt-text is first classified by its language using a pre-existing LID model ( https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06893 ).
Language-Specific Matching: Concepts are identified using metadata specific to the identified language.
Dynamic Balancing: The algorithm computes a language-specific threshold, t_lang, to balance frequent ("head") and rare ("tail") concepts. It first calculates a global target tail proportion ( p ) from the well-understood English data, then reverse-calculates the t_lang needed to achieve this same proportion in each non-English language. The probability of sampling a tail concept is 1.0, while for a head concept it is t_lang / entry_count[lang].
3. The Worldwide Training Framework
Finally, the training process itself is adapted to leverage the curated worldwide data:
Proportional Scaling of Seen Pairs: The global batch size was increased by 2.3x (from 32,768 to 75,366), ensuring the model sees a consistent amount of English data while also benefiting from cross-lingual learning.
Multilingual Tokenizer: The standard English tokenizer is replaced with a multilingual one, with XLM-V ( https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.10472 ) providing the strongest results (Table 3).
Minimal Viable Model Capacity: A key finding is that model capacity is a critical factor. This suggests the "curse" is not an inherent trade-off but a resource problem; smaller models may lack the parametric "space" to learn distinct representations for hundreds of languages without interference. The authors identified a ViT-H/14 as the "inflection point" where the model has sufficient capacity to process the diverse worldwide data and achieve mutual benefits between languages (Figure 1).
Experimental Results: Breaking the Curse
The experimental results are compelling and validate the recipe's effectiveness.
Mutual Benefit Achieved: The flagship Meta CLIP 2 ViT-H/14 model, trained on 29B worldwide pairs, achieved 81.3% zero-shot accuracy on ImageNet. This is 0.8% higher than its English-only counterpart, definitively breaking the curse of multilinguality (Table 1).
New Multilingual State-of-the-Art: The same model set new SOTA performance on a suite of challenging multilingual benchmarks without any system-level tricks. It achieved 57.4% on CVQA, 50.2% on Babel-ImageNet, and 64.3% on XM3600 image-to-text retrieval, significantly outperforming prior models like mSigLIP ( https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15343 ) and SigLIP 2 ( https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14786 ).
Enhanced Cultural Diversity: The inclusive approach led to tangible improvements on benchmarks designed to measure cultural and geographic diversity, such as Dollar Street and GeoDE (Table 4, Figure 3).
Impact and Future Directions
This work marks a significant milestone in the development of vision-language AI. By providing a transparent, reproducible, and highly effective recipe for worldwide scaling, the authors have paved a path for moving beyond the current English-centric paradigm. The key insight—that the "curse of multilinguality" is not a fundamental law but a symptom of insufficient scaling —transforms a perceived trade-off into a powerful synergy.
As the community grapples with the potential exhaustion of high-quality English data, this work unlocks the rest of the web as a viable resource for training next-generation foundation models. The open-sourced metadata, code, and model will undoubtedly accelerate research into more capable and globally inclusive MLLMs, image generation systems, and other downstream applications. This paper offers a valuable contribution to the field, providing a robust blueprint for the future of multimodal AI.
